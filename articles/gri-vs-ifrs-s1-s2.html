1. The Big Picture

If you are new to sustainability reporting, the first thing to understand is this: GRI and IFRS S1/S2 are not competing versions of the same thing. They are designed to answer different questions.

A very simple way to remember the difference:

FrameworkThe Core Question
GRI"What impacts does this company have on people, the environment, and the economy?"
IFRS S1/S2"Which sustainability and climate-related risks and opportunities could affect the company's financial prospects — and therefore matter to investors?"

That single distinction explains much of the difference. The Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) Standards help organizations report their most significant impacts on the economy, environment, and people, including human rights. The International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), under the IFRS Foundation, developed IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 to provide sustainability-related financial disclosures for investors, lenders, and other creditors.

GRI = Impact Perspective  ·  IFRS S1/S2 = Investor/Financial Perspective. There is considerable overlap between them, and the two systems are increasingly designed to work together rather than separately. In May 2026, GRI and the IFRS Foundation reaffirmed that their standards have distinct purposes but can provide complementary information.

2. What Is Sustainability Reporting?

Before comparing GRI and IFRS S1/S2, it helps to be clear on what sustainability reporting actually means. Imagine a manufacturing company with 500 employees and 3 factories. It has electricity, fuel, and water consumption; waste, suppliers, and customers; emissions, workplace accidents, and community relationships; human-rights issues, climate risks, and environmental regulations; and sustainability targets.

The company is generating information about all of this. A sustainability report essentially answers: what sustainability-related things are happening in and around the company, what impact do they have, how is the company managing them, and what does this mean for the company's future?

Different reporting standards simply give different instructions about what to identify, what is important, what to measure, what to disclose, and who the information is primarily intended for.

3. The Two Different Questions

This is the most important concept in this entire topic. Imagine a factory releases polluted wastewater into a nearby river. There are two ways of looking at this.

Perspective A — GRI (Impact)

GRI asks: what impact does this wastewater discharge have on the environment and people? Perhaps the river ecosystem is damaged, local communities lose access to clean water, fish populations decline, and nearby farmers are affected. GRI is interested in these impacts even if they do not immediately affect the company's profits.

Perspective B — IFRS S1 (Financial)

IFRS S1 asks: could this issue reasonably be expected to affect the company's cash flows, access to finance, or cost of capital? Pollution could bring regulatory fines, higher treatment costs, lawsuits, lost customers, higher insurance, and reputational damage — now the issue has a potential financial consequence.

The key is not GRI OR IFRS S1/S2. It is often GRI AND IFRS S1/S2. The GRI and IFRS Foundation have explicitly been working toward interoperability so organizations can use both systems together while recognizing their different purposes.

5. Understanding Materiality — The Most Important Difference

The word materiality creates enormous confusion in ESG reporting. The word sounds the same, but GRI and IFRS use it differently.

5.1 GRI: Impact Materiality

Under GRI, you ask: how significant is the company's impact on people, the environment, and the economy? Example: a factory employs 2,000 workers and discovers some suppliers are paying workers below legally required wages. Even with no significant financial effect on the company today, this may be a significant impact on people — and therefore material under GRI.

5.2 IFRS S1: Financial Materiality

IFRS S1 asks a different question: could information about this sustainability-related risk or opportunity reasonably be expected to influence decisions of the primary users of general-purpose financial reports? In practical terms — could this sustainability issue affect the company's financial prospects? IFRS S1 focuses specifically on risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect cash flows, access to finance, or cost of capital over the short, medium, or long term.

6. A Very Simple Example of Materiality

Company A has a factory that uses 100,000 litres of water per day, located in an area where local communities already face water scarcity.

Under GRI

Ask: is the company's water use causing a significant impact on the environment or local people? If yes → potentially material under GRI.

Under IFRS S1

Ask: could water scarcity affect production, costs, revenues, supply chain, or access to finance? If shortages could force shutdowns → potentially material under IFRS S1 too.

Now imagine the company has unlimited, low-cost water access with no regulatory restrictions and no expected production impact. It may still be material under GRI because of the environmental impact — but it may not meet the financial materiality threshold under IFRS S1. That is the difference.

7. Think of Materiality as Two Lenses

Lens 1 — GRI

What significant impacts do we have on people? Environment? Economy? Human rights? → GRI material topics.

Lens 2 — IFRS S1/S2

What sustainability risks and opportunities could affect cash flows? Access to finance? Cost of capital? Business strategy? Enterprise prospects? → ISSB material sustainability-related information.

8. What Exactly Is GRI?

GRI stands for the Global Reporting Initiative. The GRI Standards are organized into three major groups, described as an interconnected system of Universal, Sector, and Topic Standards.

1

Universal Standards

GRI 1 (Foundation), GRI 2 (General Disclosures), GRI 3 (Material Topics) — apply to every company.

2

Sector Standards

Standards relating to particular sectors, e.g. oil and gas, agriculture.

3

Topic Standards

Standards for particular topics — energy, emissions, water, biodiversity, health & safety, human rights, anti-corruption.

9. The GRI Reporting Process — Step by Step

Suppose you are the ESG officer of a manufacturing company and have been asked to "prepare our GRI sustainability report." Here is how that unfolds in practice.

1
Understand the organizationCollect information on products, factories, employees, locations, suppliers, customers, energy, water, waste, emissions, and communities. You cannot determine material topics without understanding what the company actually does.
2
Understand activities & relationshipsDon't look only at the factory — look at the entire value chain, from raw materials through suppliers, manufacturing, transportation, customers, and end of product life.
3
Identify impactsEnvironmental (GHG emissions, water, waste, biodiversity), social (health & safety, wages, human rights, community), and economic (employment, local procurement, taxes) impacts.
4
Determine which impacts are significantPrioritize using scale (how severe), scope (how widespread), and irremediable character (how difficult to repair). For potential negative impacts, likelihood is also considered.
5
Convert impacts into material topicsE.g. "Employees injured at work" → Occupational Health and Safety; "Excessive water consumption" → Water and Effluents.
6
Decide which GRI Topic Standards applyOnce material topics are identified, determine the relevant Topic Standards and collect the required information.
7
Report how each topic is managedGRI doesn't just ask "what is your number?" — it asks what you're doing about it: impacts, policies, commitments, actions, targets, and performance.

10. Understanding IFRS S1

IFRS S1 is called General Requirements for Disclosure of Sustainability-related Financial Information. It is the umbrella standard covering sustainability-related risks and opportunities broadly — climate, water, and resource availability; human capital, labour, and supply-chain issues; technology, reputation, and regulatory changes. But the key question remains: could this issue reasonably be expected to affect the company's prospects?

IFRS S1 became effective for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024, with earlier application permitted provided IFRS S2 is also applied.

11. IFRS S2 — The Climate Standard

IFRS S2 is much easier to remember: IFRS S1 covers all sustainability-related risks and opportunities, while IFRS S2 covers climate-related risks and opportunities specifically. IFRS S2 is designed to be applied together with IFRS S1 — S1 is general, S2 is climate.

12. The Four Pillars of IFRS S1/S2

One of the easiest ways to understand ISSB reporting is through four questions.

1

Governance — who oversees sustainability risks and opportunities?

2

Strategy — how does the issue affect strategy and business model?

3

Risk Management — identification, assessment, response, monitoring

4

Metrics & Targets — tCO₂e, energy, water, turnover, target progress

Example under Strategy: a textile manufacturer facing water scarcity invests in water-efficient machinery and changes suppliers.

13. IFRS S2 Goes Deeper into Climate

A company reporting under IFRS S2 typically identifies two categories of climate risk:

  • Physical risk — a physical effect of climate change (flooding, extreme heat, drought, storms, rising sea levels).
  • Transition risk — a risk arising from the transition to a lower-carbon economy (carbon pricing, new regulations, customer preferences, technological change).

The company must consider how these risks and opportunities affect its business prospects.

14. A Practical Example: A Pakistani Export Manufacturer

Let's make everything practical. Imagine PakManufacture Ltd., a manufacturer of surgical instruments for export — 1,000 employees, metal-processing operations, high consumption of electricity, natural gas, and water, generating wastewater and hazardous waste, with international customers and a complex supply chain.

Step 1 — GRI Approach

Start with: what impacts does PakManufacture have?

AreaPotential Impact
EmployeesWorkplace injuries, excessive working hours
SuppliersHuman-rights risks
EnvironmentEnergy consumption, GHG emissions, water consumption, wastewater, hazardous waste
CommunityLocal environmental impacts
CustomersProduct safety

After assessing significance, the company identifies its most significant potential GRI material topics: occupational health and safety, energy and emissions, water and effluents, waste, and human rights in the supply chain.

Step 2 — IFRS S1/S2 Approach

Now ask: which sustainability-related risks and opportunities could affect PakManufacture's prospects? Could extreme heat affect factory productivity? Could flooding disrupt transportation? Could electricity prices increase? Could customers require lower-carbon products? Could carbon regulations affect exports or financing?

Risk: Extreme heat → Workers become less productive → Production declines → Revenue decreases
Risk: High emissions → Customers demand lower-carbon suppliers → Company may lose contracts → Revenue decreases

These are exactly the kinds of financial connections IFRS S1/S2 wants disclosed.

The Key Difference in the Same Example

Consider worker safety. GRI asks: what impact does the company's operation have on worker health and safety? IFRS S1 asks: could workforce safety issues affect the company's prospects — through compensation costs, lawsuits, or disruption?

One issue can be material under both. Example — climate change: under GRI, company emissions affect the environment; under IFRS S2, climate-related transition risk could affect revenues and costs.
Some issues are material under GRI but not IFRS S1. A factory's significant impact on a local ecosystem may carry no significant financial consequence for the company.
Some issues are material under IFRS S1/S2 but less significant under GRI. An asset becoming obsolete because of a low-carbon transition creates impairment risk — highly relevant under IFRS S1/S2.

15. The "ESG to Finance" Bridge

This is where accounting and finance professionals have a major advantage. Think of it as a chain:

Sustainability issue → Risk or opportunity → Business model/strategy → Cash flows → Access to finance → Cost of capital → Company's prospects

Example 1 (water scarcity): water scarcity → production disruption → lower output → lower sales → lower cash flows.

Example 2 (carbon regulation): carbon regulation → higher compliance costs → higher operating expenses → lower margins → lower cash flows.

This is a genuinely useful way for accountants and finance professionals to understand ISSB.

16. A Practical Reporting Workflow for Using Both

Now suppose you are hired as the ESG reporting consultant, and the company wants to use GRI + IFRS S1 + IFRS S2 together.

1
Understand the businessBusiness model, operations, products, locations, value chain, employees, suppliers, and customers.
2
Build the sustainability universeCreate a long list of possible issues — environmental, social, governance — without deciding materiality yet.
3
Conduct the GRI impact assessmentEngage stakeholders and experts, assess significance of impacts, identify GRI material topics.
4
Conduct the ISSB assessmentFor every major issue, ask whether it could reasonably affect prospects — revenue, expenses, assets, liabilities, cash flows, financing, cost of capital, strategy, continuity.
5
Climate assessmentApply IFRS S2 to climate issues — identify physical risks, transition risks, and opportunities.
6
Determine required metricsFor each material issue: what to measure, where the data comes from, who owns it, the methodology, and the evidence.
7
Governance and controlsEstablish a clear line of accountability — data owner → collection → calculation → review → approval → disclosure.
8
Draft the reportGRI: significant impacts and how they're managed. IFRS S1: material risks/opportunities and their effect on prospects. IFRS S2: climate-related disclosures.

18 · TAKEAWAY

The Most Important Takeaway

Don't memorize a list of disclosures. Instead, understand the logic.

GRI logic: What do we do? → What impacts result? → Who/what is affected? → How significant are those impacts? → Which impacts are material? → How do we manage them? → What do we disclose?

IFRS S1/S2 logic: What sustainability/climate issues exist? → Could they affect our prospects? → What risks and opportunities arise? → How do they affect our strategy/business model? → How does management identify and manage them? → What metrics and targets demonstrate performance? → What would investors need to know?

GRI is primarily about the company's impacts on the world; IFRS S1/S2 are primarily about how sustainability and climate-related matters can affect the company. Once this distinction becomes clear, the rest of the standards become much easier to learn.
